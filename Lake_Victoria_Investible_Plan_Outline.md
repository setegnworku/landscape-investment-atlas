**Landscape Investment Atlas | Pilot example**  
**Version:** Draft outline for team development  
**Purpose:** COP17-ready proof of concept — from territorial space to customized investment plan  
**Status:** Outline for review (content to be validated with basin partners and updated data)

## Executive summary (1 page)

**Investment thesis:** The Lake Victoria Basin is one of Africa's most important water, fisheries and food systems. Its value is eroding through catchment degradation, pollution, wetland loss and fisheries decline — but coordinated landscape investment can protect existing value, restore critical assets and unlock a blue-economy pathway for ~35 million people.

**Why invest now:** Illustrative cost of inaction **US$600M–1,000M** vs. strategic investment **US$100M–150M** with illustrative **L-ROI 3.0–8.0×** (medium–high confidence).

**What this plan proposes:** A sequenced package of **protect**, **create**, **enable** and **scale** investments across water, fisheries, wetlands and basin governance — financed through a mix of MDBs, governments, water funds and blue-economy investors.

**Primary beneficiaries:** Fishing communities, basin agriculture, urban water users, national economies (Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, and riparian states), and global public goods (biodiversity, climate resilience).

## 1. Landscape profile

### 1.1 Geographic and territorial scope
- **Name:** Lake Victoria Basin (Living Landscape)
- **Scope:** Transboundary lake and watershed system (~194,200 km²)
- **Riparian context:** Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania (plus broader Nile Basin linkages)
- **Population:** ~35 million people directly dependent on basin services
- **Dominant systems:** Freshwater lake, tributaries, wetlands, agriculture, inland fisheries, urban-industrial corridors

### 1.2 Socio-ecological characteristics
- Africa's largest lake by area; anchor of regional food security and livelihoods
- Major inland fishery (~1 million tonnes/year landings; beach value **US$550M–840M/year** in cited period)
- Wetlands provide flood regulation, water-quality buffering, biodiversity habitat
- Catchment pressures: erosion, sedimentation, nutrient loading, encroachment, climate variability
- Governance: multi-country basin institutions, national agencies, local cooperatives and communities

### 1.3 Investment thesis (one paragraph)
> The Lake Victoria Basin generates disproportionate regional value through water security, fisheries and agricultural productivity. That value is at risk from degrading catchments, declining fish stocks and wetland loss. A landscape-scale investment package that combines watershed protection, pollution reduction, fisheries governance, wetland restoration and strengthened basin coordination can protect hundreds of millions of dollars in existing value, create new blue-economy opportunities, and deliver multifunctional returns across water, food, livelihoods and biodiversity.

### 1.4 Comparative advantage
- **Water security & blue economy** at scale
- **Very high** investor fit for water funds, MDBs and development finance
- **High** fit for biodiversity, climate and impact investors
- Strong evidence base relative to other Living Landscapes in the portfolio

## 3. Value at risk and cost of inaction

### 3.1 Baseline value (evidence anchors)
- **Fisheries:** ~1M t/year landings; beach value rising from ~US$550M (2011) to ~US$840M (2014); ~220,000 fishers; ~US$600M annual wealth (IFAD-cited figures)
- **Livelihoods:** Millions of indirect beneficiaries along fisheries and agricultural value chains
- **Wetlands:** Economically valued regulating services for lake water quality
- **Biodiversity:** Documented willingness-to-pay for conservation (order of magnitude: hundreds of millions KES/year in cited study)

### 3.2 Value at risk (VAR)
- Continued degradation of catchments → sedimentation, treatment costs, lost hydrological regulation
- Fisheries decline → nutrition, income and employment losses across riparian communities
- Wetland loss → reduced flood buffering, water-quality decline, biodiversity loss

### 3.3 Cost of inaction (illustrative)
- **US$600M–1,000M** cumulative value at risk under business-as-usual / delayed intervention (Atlas illustrative range)
- Interpretation: losses accumulate through declining productivity, service erosion and increasing vulnerability — often invisible in sectoral budgets until crisis points

### 3.4 Urgency narrative
- Fisheries and water assets are **declining now** with **high-confidence** evidence
- Protection investments avoid losses that exceed many restoration costs
- Transboundary coordination failures amplify local degradation

## 5. Action design — multifunctionality, synergies and trade-offs

### 5.1 Sequenced action package (draft logic)

**Phase 1 — Enable (Years 0–2)**
- Basin investment platform and governance upgrade
- Shared monitoring framework (water quality, fisheries, wetlands)
- Community and stakeholder validation of priorities and safeguards

**Phase 2 — Protect (Years 1–5)**
- Priority catchment protection in high-sediment / high-nutrient sub-basins
- Fisheries governance (gear, effort, habitat zones)
- Wetland protection in critical regulation zones

**Phase 3 — Create (Years 3–10)**
- Targeted watershed restoration and pollution controls
- Fisheries habitat restoration and value-chain investment
- Wetland rehabilitation linked to fisheries and water quality outcomes

**Phase 4 — Scale (Years 5–15)**
- Water fund or blended finance vehicle for basin-scale replication
- Blue-economy enterprise pipeline (sustainable fisheries, ecotourism, nature-based solutions)

### 5.2 Synergies
| Action cluster | Synergistic returns |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Catchment restoration + wetland protection | Water quality ↑, fisheries habitat ↑, sediment ↓ |
| Fisheries governance + community enterprises | Stocks recover + livelihoods stabilize |
| Monitoring + investor reporting | Readiness ↑, finance mobilization ↑ |

### 5.3 Trade-offs (to analyze explicitly)
| Tension | Trade-off question | How plan addresses it |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Agriculture vs. water quality | Nutrient runoff from intensification | Target agroecological practices in priority catchments |
| Fishing effort vs. stock recovery | Short-term income vs. long-term productivity | Phased governance + alternative livelihoods |
| Wetland conversion vs. regulation | Land use pressure | Protection + restoration corridors + community benefit sharing |
| National priorities vs. transboundary equity | Who pays, who benefits | Basin-level benefit-sharing and finance architecture |

### 5.4 Multifunctionality objective
- Avoid single-objective optimization (e.g. water only)
- Target balanced returns across **water, fisheries, livelihoods, biodiversity, governance**
- Use **MDI** (illustrative **2.15**) to track diversity of return streams

## 9. Monitoring, safeguards and confidence

### 9.1 Monitoring framework

| Tier | Indicators |
|------|------------|
| **Asset condition** | Water quality, wetland extent, fish biomass, catch trends |
| **Return streams** | Beach value, household income, jobs, treatment costs avoided |
| **Process** | Governance meetings, compliance rates, community participation |
| **Finance** | Funds mobilized, disbursement, leverage ratio |

### 9.2 Safeguards
- Free, prior and informed consent for community-affected interventions
- Gender and social inclusion in fisheries and value-chain investments
- No displacement without compensation and livelihood alternatives
- Environmental flow and habitat protections in restoration design
- Anti-corruption and transparent benefit-sharing in basin finance

### 9.3 Confidence and assumptions log
- Document all L-ROI inputs and ranges
- Flag data gaps (spatial prioritization, costing, additionality)
- Schedule annual recalibration as monitoring data improves
